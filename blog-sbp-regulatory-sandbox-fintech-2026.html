SBP Opens Its Second Regulatory Sandbox Cohort for AI and Fintech

The State Bank launched a second sandbox cohort focused on AI, payments, remittances and next-generation financial services.

The cohort

SBP announced the second Regulatory Sandbox cohort on October 2, 2026. Solutions can be tested in a controlled environment under regulatory oversight.

Focus areas

The cohort covers AI in financial services, payment innovations, technology-enabled inward remittances and a next-generation financial-services theme. SBP says applications are accepted until November 30, 2026.

Why a sandbox exists

It gives innovators and regulators a controlled place to test products, identify consumer-protection risks and learn what rules may be needed.

Why AI needs care

Finance requires privacy, security, governance, explainability and consumer recourse alongside technical accuracy.

What the announcement does and does not show

A policy, investment, partnership or programme announcement is not the same thing as a completed economic outcome. The first task is to identify what has actually been committed, what remains proposed, and which institution is responsible for implementation. Dates and stated targets should be preserved because they provide the baseline against which later progress can be checked.

Technology stories also benefit from separating infrastructure, skills, finance and adoption. A new programme may improve one part of the ecosystem without immediately changing productivity across the whole economy. Businesses still face practical constraints such as access to capital, reliable connectivity, trained staff, customer demand and regulatory requirements.

Why this matters for Pakistan

Digital activity increasingly connects local businesses with international markets. Software services, digital payments, artificial intelligence and technology-enabled finance can reduce transaction costs and create new routes to customers, but the benefits depend on execution. Training programmes need participants and employers; financing programmes need viable companies; and regulatory initiatives need clear rules that firms can understand.

The distribution of benefits is another useful question. Large companies may have easier access to capital and specialist staff, while smaller firms may need simpler tools, training and predictable compliance requirements. A headline investment figure therefore should not be interpreted as a guaranteed increase in employment, exports or productivity.

What to watch next

Follow-up releases should be checked for participants, money actually disbursed, projects completed, export or revenue outcomes and measurable adoption. Comparing those later observations with the original announcement provides a factual way to assess implementation without relying on promotional language alone.
